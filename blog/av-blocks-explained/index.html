AV blocks explained: first, second and third degree heart block

By Mitch, registered paramedic and founder of Brraio · 14 October 2026 · 6 min read

An AV block is a delay or a break in the signal passing from the atria to the ventricles. The trick to sorting them is to ask two things: is the PR interval changing, and does every P wave get through?

First degree AV block

The PR interval is longer than 0.20 seconds (5 small boxes), and it stays the same on every beat. Every P wave is followed by a QRS. It is a delay, not a missed beat, and it is often found by chance.

Second degree, Mobitz type 1 (Wenckebach)

The PR interval gets a little longer with each beat until one P wave is not followed by a QRS. The cycle then starts again. The usual memory hook is: "Longer, longer, longer, drop, then you have a Wenckebach." It is usually at the level of the AV node and is often benign, but always judge it against how the patient looks.

Second degree, Mobitz type 2

The PR interval stays the same, then a P wave suddenly fails to conduct. The hook is: "If some P waves don't get through, then you have a Mobitz II." The block usually sits below the AV node and can progress without warning to complete block, so it is treated more seriously than type 1.

Third degree (complete) heart block

No P waves reach the ventricles. The atria and ventricles each beat at their own pace, so P waves and QRS complexes appear to march through each other. The ventricular rate is slow, and the QRS can be wide if the escape rhythm comes from the ventricles. The hook: "If P's and Q's don't agree, you have a third degree."

Do not forget the patient

A slow rate with poor perfusion needs action, whatever the block is called. Follow your local clinical practice guidelines for the treatment. The strips here are teaching sketches, not real recordings.
