After a hard shift: looking after your mental health in healthcare

By Mitch, registered paramedic and founder of Brraio · 17 November 2026 · 5 min read

Some jobs stay with you. You finish the shift, drive home, and your brain is still on scene. That is a normal human reaction, not a sign you are in the wrong job. Here is a practical look at what helps on the day, over the following weeks, and when it is time to get more support.

What is normal after a hard job

After a confronting job, a death, a paediatric case or a shift that just would not stop, it is common to notice things like:

  • Replaying the job, or thinking "what if I had..."
  • Trouble getting to sleep, or vivid dreams
  • Feeling flat, numb, teary or short-tempered
  • Being on edge, or jumpy at certain sounds or smells
  • Not wanting to talk about it, or wanting to talk about nothing else

For many people these reactions ease over the following days to weeks. Noticing them is not weakness. It is information.

On the day: the basics first

  • Talk to someone who gets it. A quick check-in with your crew, a colleague or a supervisor after the job can help. You do not have to go into detail.
  • Look after your body. Eat something, drink water, and get outside or move if you can. Your nervous system has been running hot.
  • Make a buffer before sleep. A shower, a walk, music or a routine that marks the end of the shift. Try to keep screens and the news out of the last half hour.
  • Go easy on alcohol. It can feel like it takes the edge off, but it tends to make sleep and mood worse.
  • It is OK not to talk. Some people process by talking, some by doing. Neither is wrong.

Over the next few weeks

Keep an eye on how you are tracking. Keep up the routines that keep you well: sleep where shift work allows, exercise, time with people outside work, and things you enjoy that have nothing to do with healthcare.

If your workplace has peer support or an Employee Assistance Program (EAP), use it. These services are usually confidential and they exist for exactly this. You do not need to be in crisis to call.

When to get more help

Reach out for more support if:

  • Things are not easing after a few weeks, or are getting worse
  • It is affecting your sleep, work, relationships or how you function day to day
  • You are avoiding work, places or people because of what happened
  • You are relying on alcohol or other drugs to cope
  • You are having thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life

Your GP (family doctor) is a good place to start. They can talk through options with you, including a referral to a psychologist. If you are having thoughts of suicide, please contact one of the services below today.

Looking out for a mate

You will often notice a colleague is not themselves before they do. You do not need the perfect words. R U OK? suggests four simple steps: ask them directly, listen without judging or trying to fix it, encourage action such as talking to their GP or a support service, and check in again later. If you are worried about their safety, stay with them and call for help.

Students and volunteers count too

You do not have to be on the payroll for a job to affect you. If you are a student on placement or a volunteer, talk to your clinical supervisor, your university's counselling service or your organisation's support contact, and use the services below.

Where to get support now

If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call your local emergency number now: 000 in Australia, 911 in the US and Canada, 999 or 112 in the UK and Ireland, 111 in New Zealand, 995 in Singapore, or 112 from a mobile in South Africa.

In Australia

  • Lifeline: 13 11 14, 24 hours, or lifeline.org.au
  • Suicide Call Back Service: 1300 659 467, 24 hours
  • Beyond Blue: 1300 22 4636, or beyondblue.org.au
  • 13YARN (for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people): 13 92 76, 24 hours
  • R U OK?: tips for starting a conversation at ruok.org.au
  • Black Dog Institute: information and tools at blackdoginstitute.org.au

Outside Australia

  • US: 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, call or text 988, 24 hours, or 988lifeline.org
  • Canada: call or text 9-8-8, 24 hours, or 988.ca
  • UK and Ireland: Samaritans, 116 123, 24 hours, or samaritans.org
  • New Zealand: Need to talk? Call or text 1737, 24 hours
  • Singapore: Samaritans of Singapore (SOS), 1767, 24 hours
  • South Africa: SADAG Suicide Crisis Helpline, 0800 567 567
  • Anywhere else: find a free, confidential helpline in your country at findahelpline.com

This article is general information, not medical or mental health advice. If you are worried about your own or someone else's wellbeing, talk to your GP or one of the services above.
