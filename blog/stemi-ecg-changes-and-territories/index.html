STEMI on the ECG: ST elevation, territories and common mimics

By Mitch, registered paramedic and founder of Brraio · 14 October 2026 · 7 min read

Finding an ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) quickly is one of the most important ECG skills for a paramedic, nurse or doctor. Here is a clear way to look for it, and the traps that catch people out.

What a STEMI can look like, in order

  1. Minutes: tall, broad "hyperacute" T waves.
  2. Hours: ST elevation, often with reciprocal ST depression in the opposite leads.
  3. Hours to days: Q waves form and T waves invert.
  4. Days to weeks: the ST segment settles, and Q waves usually remain.

Not every STEMI looks textbook, and early recordings can look almost normal. Repeat the ECG if the story fits and symptoms are ongoing.

How much ST elevation counts?

One commonly used definition is new J-point elevation of at least 1 mm in two contiguous leads, with higher thresholds in V2 and V3 that vary with age and sex. Guidelines differ between services, so use the criteria in your local protocol.

Which leads show which wall?

Groups of leads that look at the same wall are called contiguous. ST elevation in two or more contiguous leads is the key pattern. In an inferior STEMI, check for right ventricle involvement with right-sided leads, and be careful with nitrates, as your protocol will advise.

Reciprocal changes

ST depression in the leads that look at the opposite wall supports a true STEMI. For example, inferior ST elevation often comes with ST depression in aVL.

Common mimics

  • Pericarditis: widespread ST elevation, often with PR depression. There is no reciprocal ST depression, except in aVR (and sometimes V1), which often also shows PR elevation.
  • Left bundle branch block: the wide QRS changes the ST segment and T waves, which makes interpretation harder. A new LBBB with a typical story needs urgent thought.
  • Early repolarisation: a common normal variant in younger people.
  • Left ventricular hypertrophy and high potassium: both can change the ST segment and T waves.

Put the story first

An ECG that looks worrying in a patient with no symptoms, or a normal ECG in a patient with a typical story, are both reminders that the ECG is one piece of the picture. Our 8-step ECG method keeps the rest of the read tidy so ST changes do not get missed.
