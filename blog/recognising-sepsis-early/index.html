Clinical refresher

Recognising sepsis early: a practical refresher

By Mitch, registered paramedic and founder of Brraio · 23 November 2026 · 5 min read

Sepsis rarely announces itself. It often looks like a chest infection, a UTI or "just a bit off" until suddenly it is not. The earlier someone thinks "could this be sepsis?", the better the patient tends to do. Here is a practical refresher on what sepsis is, who is at risk, the signs to look for and what to do when you are worried.

What sepsis actually is

Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. In plain words: the infection is the trigger, but it is the body's own response that starts damaging organs. That is why a patient can look far sicker than the infection you can see would suggest.

Septic shock is the severe end: sepsis with circulatory failure, where the patient needs vasopressors to keep their blood pressure up and has a raised lactate even after adequate fluids.

Sepsis is not a single test result. It is a clinical picture: a likely infection plus signs that organs are starting to struggle.

Who is at higher risk

Anyone can get sepsis, but keep it higher on your list for:

  • Babies, young children and older adults
  • People with weakened immunity, such as those on chemotherapy, long-term steroids or other immune-suppressing drugs
  • People with chronic illness, such as diabetes, kidney, liver or lung disease
  • Recent surgery, an invasive procedure or a recent hospital stay
  • Indwelling lines, catheters or other devices
  • People who are pregnant or have recently given birth

Common sources of infection

When you suspect sepsis, think about where the infection might be coming from. Common sources include the chest (pneumonia), the urinary tract, the abdomen, skin and soft tissue (cellulitis, wounds, pressure areas), lines and devices, and less commonly the brain and spinal cord (meningitis). Sometimes you will not find a source at all, and that does not rule sepsis out.

The signs to look for

Many of the clues are in a normal set of observations. The trick is to take them seriously, together.

  • Breathing fast. A raised respiratory rate is one of the most useful and most often missed signs. Count it properly, for a full 30 to 60 seconds.
  • Heart rate up. Tachycardia is common. On the monitor this is often sinus tachycardia, but sepsis can also tip people into new arrhythmias such as AF.
  • Blood pressure down, or lower than normal for that person. A normal blood pressure does not rule sepsis out, especially early on, because the body compensates.
  • New confusion or drowsiness. Any change in mental state is a red flag. Ask family or carers what is normal for this person.
  • Temperature high or low. Fever is common, but a low temperature can be a worse sign, and some patients have a normal temperature.
  • Skin and perfusion. Mottled, pale, cool or clammy skin, and a slow capillary refill.
  • Passing little or no urine.
  • A rash that does not blanch. Treat this as an emergency until proven otherwise.

Patients often tell you something is wrong in their own words: "I have never felt this sick" or "I feel like I am going to die". Listen to that.

Screening tools: useful, not the final word

Most services use an early warning score or a sepsis screening tool, such as NEWS2 or a local equivalent. These turn a set of observations into a trigger for escalation, and they work best when you use them every time, not only when you are already worried.

You may also see qSOFA, a quick bedside prompt that scores one point each for a respiratory rate of 22 or more, altered mental state, and a systolic blood pressure of 100 or less. Two or more points flags a patient at higher risk of a poor outcome. A low score does not rule sepsis out, so do not let it talk you out of what you are seeing.

Follow the tool and thresholds in your service's clinical practice guidelines, because they differ between services and countries.

The patients who catch us out

  • Older adults, who may have no fever and present with a fall, new confusion or "just not themselves".
  • People on beta blockers, which can blunt the rise in heart rate you would expect.
  • Immunosuppressed patients, who may look surprisingly well right up until they do not.
  • Children, who compensate well and then deteriorate quickly. Lethargy, poor feeding, fewer wet nappies and a slow capillary refill matter. So does a parent or carer telling you their child is not right.
  • The "simple UTI" or "chest infection", especially when the observations do not fit a simple infection.

What to do when you are worried

The single most important step is to escalate early. Sepsis care is time critical, and treatment usually works best when it starts early. Depending on your role and setting, that might mean calling for senior review, activating a sepsis pathway, pre-notifying the receiving hospital or calling for backup.

Your service's clinical practice guidelines will set out exactly what to do, but early sepsis care commonly involves:

  • Supporting airway, breathing and circulation, including oxygen if the patient needs it
  • Repeating a full set of observations often, and watching the trend
  • Getting IV access, and taking blood tests, including cultures and lactate, where that is part of your role and setting
  • Giving antibiotics early, as your guidelines direct
  • Giving fluids for low blood pressure or poor perfusion, with careful reassessment
  • Finding and treating the source of infection
  • Monitoring urine output

Say the word "sepsis"

When you hand over or escalate, say it out loud: "I am worried this patient has sepsis." Naming it focuses everyone on the same problem and helps make sure the next team acts quickly. A structured handover helps here: who the patient is, what you are worried about, the observations and trend, what you have done, and what you need.

If you are a student or volunteer, you do not need to be certain. Telling your supervisor "these obs worry me" is exactly the right move.

Quick recap

  • Sepsis is organ dysfunction caused by the body's response to infection.
  • Count the respiratory rate properly, and treat new confusion as a red flag.
  • Normal blood pressure or no fever does not rule it out.
  • Use your screening tool every time, but trust your clinical concern too.
  • Escalate early, say "sepsis" at handover, and follow your service's guidelines.

This article is general education for health workers and students, not medical advice. Always follow your training, your service's clinical practice guidelines and the advice of senior clinicians. If you think someone may have sepsis, seek urgent medical help.
